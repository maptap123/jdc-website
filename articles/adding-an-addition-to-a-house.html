Adding an addition to a house is one of the biggest projects a homeowner ever takes on. Done right, it disappears into the original home and you forget it was ever new. Done wrong, it always looks tacked on. Here is the full process we walk clients through in Southeast Indiana.

Quick Answer

Adding an addition to a house in Southeast Indiana typically takes 4 to 8 months from first sketch to move-in day, and runs $200 to $500 per square foot depending on scope. The process moves through six phases: design, permits, site prep, framing and dry-in, interior finish, and final walkthrough. Skipping any one of them is where projects go sideways.

What "Adding an Addition" Actually Means

An addition is any new heated, permitted square footage attached to your existing home. That covers a lot of ground: a primary suite off the back, a kitchen bump-out, a second story over a garage, an in-law wing, a sunroom that's insulated for year-round use.

What it is not: a detached shed, a screened porch that stays cold in winter, or a garage conversion where nothing gets added to the footprint. Those are their own category and follow a different process.

Every real addition ties into your existing structure, roof, HVAC, plumbing, and electrical. That's the part most homeowners don't see coming. You're not just building a new room. You're renovating the wall and roof it connects to, and often the systems that feed it.

The Six Phases, Start to Finish

Here's how the project actually unfolds. The timelines assume a typical 300 to 600 square foot addition in Dearborn County or the Cincinnati tri-state area.

Phase 1 · Design & Feasibility

This is where most projects live or die. Before any drawings, we walk the property and check the boring things: setbacks, easements, septic location, where the load-bearing walls run, whether your HVAC can handle another room. About 1 in 5 homeowners find out at this stage that the addition they pictured won't fit where they wanted it.

Design itself takes 4 to 8 weeks for most additions. You'll go through 2 or 3 rounds of drawings before anything gets built. Rush this and you'll pay to fix it in framing.

Phase 2 · Permits & Approvals

In Dearborn County, addition permits typically come back in 2 to 4 weeks. If you're in a subdivision with an HOA, add another 2 to 6 weeks for architectural review. Homes on septic need a health department sign-off if the addition changes bedroom count.

Phase 3 · Site Prep & Foundation

Excavation, footings, foundation walls, and slab or crawl. This is 2 to 4 weeks depending on soil conditions and whether you're going over a walk-out grade. Wet spring weather in Southeast Indiana adds delay here more than anywhere else in the schedule.

Phase 4 · Framing & Dry-In

The moment the addition starts to feel real. Walls go up, roof gets framed and shingled, windows and doors get set. Once you're dry-in, weather stops being a problem. This phase runs 3 to 6 weeks.

This is also when we cut into the existing house. It's the loudest, dustiest week of the whole project, and it's the week you'll wish you'd moved out for.

Phase 5 · Interior Finish

Mechanicals rough-in, insulation, drywall, trim, flooring, paint, cabinets, fixtures. This is the longest single phase: 6 to 12 weeks depending on the finish level and whether we're matching existing millwork or hardwood floors. Matching is always slower than starting fresh.

Phase 6 · Final Walkthrough & Punch List

Final inspections, a walkthrough with the homeowner, and a punch list that usually takes 1 to 2 weeks to close out. This is where a good contractor earns their reputation. The last 5% of the job is 50% of the client experience.

What Homeowners Don't Plan For

After three decades of adding onto homes in Dearborn County and the Cincinnati area, these are the surprises that catch homeowners off guard almost every time.

  • The HVAC upgrade. Your existing furnace and AC were sized for the original square footage. Add 500 feet and you often need a new unit, not just a duct extension.
  • Matching the exterior. Old brick, old siding, old roof shingles. Sometimes matching them costs more than starting fresh on the whole side of the house.
  • The electrical panel. Homes built before 2005 often have a 100 or 150 amp panel that can't take a new circuit load. Panel upgrades run $2,500 to $4,500.
  • Living through construction. Most families stay in the house during an addition. Plan for 2 to 3 weeks where a wall of your home is open plastic sheeting.
  • The septic question. Adding a bedroom on a septic system usually triggers a re-evaluation. Sometimes a bigger tank or leach field. Budget for it or design around it.
  • Property taxes. A completed addition gets reassessed. In Dearborn County, expect your annual tax bill to go up by roughly 1% of the added value.

Questions to Ask Before You Start

These are the questions we wish more homeowners asked us before signing a contract, not six months in.

  • Is my contractor pulling the permits? They should be. If they ask you to pull them, that's a red flag.
  • What is the fixed price vs. the allowance? Allowances are placeholders. If cabinets are an "allowance," you'll pay the actual number, not the estimate.
  • How do you handle change orders? They should be written, priced, and signed before any work happens. No verbal upgrades.
  • What's the warranty? One year on workmanship is the industry minimum. We offer longer on structural.
  • Who's on site every day? There should be one lead carpenter or project manager who owns your job start to finish.

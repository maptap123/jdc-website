House Addition Plans:
How to Choose the Right Layout.

JDC Construction · Lawrenceburg, Indiana 6 min read
JDC Construction › Articles › House Addition Plans: How to Choose the Right Layout for Your Home

A great house addition starts with the right plan, not the biggest one. The best plans match your family's daily routine, your home's bones, and your lot. Get that trio right and the rest of the project gets a lot easier.

Quick Answer

House addition plans fall into five main layouts: bump-outs, full-room additions, second-story adds, primary suite wings, and in-law suites. The right one depends on your lot, foundation, and what's actually missing from your daily routine. In Southeast Indiana, most families land on a 300 to 700 square foot addition that adds one or two useful rooms, costs $90,000 to $250,000+, and takes 4 to 7 months from signed plans to move-in.

Start With the Problem, Not the Plan

Most homeowners come to us with a size in mind. "We want to add 500 square feet." That's a budget conversation, not a plan conversation.

A good set of house addition plans starts somewhere else: what isn't working in your current layout. Are you tripping over backpacks every morning? Is the primary bedroom on a different floor from your young kids? Does the kitchen dead-end at a wall when you want to cook, host, and watch the game at the same time?

The right plan solves a specific problem. It's not just extra square footage. In a Southeast Indiana ranch, that often means a bump-out off the kitchen or a primary suite on the back of the house. In a 1940s Lawrenceburg two-story, it usually means pushing the kitchen into a new family room or stacking a second story over the garage.

The Five Most Common House Addition Plan Types

Every addition we build in Dearborn County and the Cincinnati area fits one of these five shapes. Picking the right one up front saves weeks in design and tens of thousands in rework.

Bump-Out (50 – 200 sq ft)

A small extension off an existing room, usually a kitchen, dining room, or primary bath. Bump-outs often cantilever off the existing floor system, so you may not need a full foundation. Best for homes with tight lots or short wish lists.

Full Room Addition (250 – 500 sq ft)

A single new room — family room, first-floor bedroom, mudroom plus pantry — tied onto the back or side of the house on its own foundation. The workhorse of Southeast Indiana additions.

Primary Suite Addition (400 – 700 sq ft)

A new primary bedroom, bathroom, and walk-in closet, usually on the main floor. Popular with homeowners who want to age in place without selling and starting over.

Second-Story Addition (600 – 1,200 sq ft)

New bedrooms and a bath built on top of the existing footprint. Great when your lot is tight but your foundation can carry the load. Requires structural review of the original walls and floor system.

In-Law Suite or Attached Apartment (500 – 900 sq ft)

A self-contained addition with a bedroom, bath, and often a kitchenette and separate entry. Useful for multigenerational families or long-term guests. Dearborn County zoning allows these in most residential districts, but setbacks and septic capacity need to be checked early.

Designing a Layout That Flows With Your House

Bad addition plans look like they were taped onto the house. Good ones feel like they were always there. That difference is almost entirely about how the new space ties into the existing floor plan.

A few things we check on every set of house addition plans before the first wall gets framed:

  • Where you enter the new space. If you walk through a bedroom to reach a family room, the plan has failed. Every new room needs a clean path from the main living areas.
  • How the rooflines meet. A hip tied to a gable at the wrong pitch screams "addition." Matching ridge heights and pitches, or using a clear transition, keeps the house looking like one house.
  • Where load-bearing walls are. Opening a wall between the old house and the addition usually means a beam. That beam dictates ceiling height and sometimes forces a soffit nobody wants.
  • Sightlines from the kitchen. On most additions, the kitchen stays put and becomes the hub between old and new. A plan that blocks the view to the new space usually feels wrong within a month of moving in.
  • Window placement. Plans drawn at a desk forget what you actually see from the sofa. We stand in the room before windows get fixed on paper.

"The best house addition plans don't look like an addition. They look like the house was built that way."

Mistakes We See in House Addition Plans

Most problems on a jobsite trace back to the plan. Here are the common ones we clean up when homeowners bring us a set drawn somewhere else.

  • Planning without measuring the existing house. A plan drawn from the tax card or an old survey is a plan that won't line up with your real walls.
  • No consideration of mechanicals. Adding 500 square feet to a house with an undersized furnace guarantees a cold room in a Southeast Indiana January.
  • Ignoring the lot. Dearborn County setbacks, septic drain fields, and well placement all limit where an addition can sit.
  • Designing around furniture you own, not the way you live. Plans should work for the next ten years, not for how the sofa fits today.
  • Skipping the "in between" rooms. Mudrooms, hallways, and transition spaces are where good additions feel seamless. Cutting them to save square footage usually backfires.

From First Sketch to a Buildable Plan

A real set of house addition plans takes time. Rushing it is where projects get expensive. Here's the path we walk most Dearborn County and Cincinnati-area homeowners through:

  • Walk the house and lot together. We see the problem on site before anyone draws a line.
  • Agree on a real budget range. Not a dream number. The one your lender and your life can live with.
  • Sketch two or three layout options. Different footprints, different costs. You react, we adjust.
  • Lock the plan before pricing the finishes. Changing the footprint in week eight costs ten times what it costs in week two.
  • Build the permit set around the final layout. Structural, mechanical, and energy details get added once the shape is settled.
